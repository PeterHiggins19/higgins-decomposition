# How to moot this talk

A moot is what law students do to learn an argument. You don't memorise the script word-for-word — you memorise **anchor phrases** that trigger each section. Saying the anchor phrase causes the next sentences to come out. The phrases link together. You can deliver a 15-minute talk from ten anchors plus the natural flow of the material.

This page tells you how to learn the talk that way. It works on a phone, on a train, in the airport, before bed. No slides needed during study — only the phrases.

> **Before you start the moot rounds,** read [SPEAKER_BRIEF.md](SPEAKER_BRIEF.md) once. The brief is the strategic compass — why you're at the lectern, what each beat is doing for the room, what each country signals in the narrative arc. The moot work is tactical; the brief tells you what the tactics are *for*.

## The moot method — five rounds

You can do all five rounds in one evening, or one round a day for five days. Both work.

### Round 1 — Just the anchors

Say all ten anchor phrases in order, out loud. Three times. Until you don't need to peek.

**Test:** can you say all ten without looking at this page? If yes, move on.

### Round 2 — Anchor + one sentence each

Now expand each anchor to one sentence. For example:

- *"Energy generation is a composition. A country's electricity mix is a vector of carrier shares that sum to one."*
- *"No monitoring framework combines all three conjuncts. Aitchison geometry, formal change detection, carrier-level attribution — combined into one observable stack."*

Say all ten as anchor-plus-sentence. Three times.

**Test:** can you do all ten as anchor + sentence without checking the README? If yes, move on.

### Round 3 — Anchor + key content

Expand to the actual content of each beat. The README has the full text — read it for the beat you're working on, then close it and try to deliver.

This is where the natural sentence flow has to come through. The anchor triggers the content; you don't memorise the sentences, you let them come.

Some content you *do* need to remember exactly:

- **MC-4 in three-conjunct form** (Beat 2): *"natively in Aitchison geometry, with formal change detection, at the carrier level — combined into one observable stack"*
- **The Germany p-value caveat** (Beat 5): *"computed against the series' own empirical-frequency baseline. This is a weaker null than a Dirichlet, permutation, or bootstrap null."*
- **The five deceptive-drift countries** (Beat 7): AUS, CHN, GBR, IND, JPN — five of nine.
- **The three open questions** (Beat 8): Q1 = concentration vs Aitchison norm. Q2 = right family of simplex distances. Q3 = right null model.
- **The two papers found in the prior-art search** (Beat 9): Morais Thomas-Agnan & Simioni 2017, and Arata & Onozaki 2017.

The rest you can paraphrase in your own words. The talk is yours.

### Round 5 — Stand-and-deliver

Stand up. Phone-free. Deliver the whole talk to an empty room, or to a single trusted listener. The first time will be rough — that's normal. The second time will be cleaner. The third time you're ready.

If you can deliver Round 5 inside the time tolerance with the anchor phrases as your only cue card, you are ready for Coimbra.

## What to say if you blank on stage

This is the moot-prep insurance policy. If your mind goes blank mid-beat:

- Look down at the cheat sheet — `CHEAT_SHEET.md`, on your phone or printed.
- Find the anchor phrase for the beat you were in.
- Say it out loud. The rest will come back.
- If it doesn't — skip to the next anchor. The audience will not notice you skipped one sub-point.

Specifically: **don't try to recover a forgotten sub-point in the middle of a beat.** Move to the next anchor. You will lose less time and look more composed.

---

## What to say if a reviewer asks a question you don't have an answer for

This is part of the talk's discipline. Three rules:

1. **Acknowledge the question is interesting.** Buy yourself five seconds.
2. **Name what you don't know.** *"That's beyond what I have ready evidence for. My honest answer is — I don't know, and I'd want to look at it carefully before claiming."*
3. **Hand it back.** *"Would you be willing to talk about it after? I'd value the pointer."*

The audience is friendly. They will respect "I don't know" far more than a bluff.

## Two-day study plan (if you have only two days)

**Day 1:**
- Round 1 (anchors only) — 15 minutes
- Round 2 (anchor + one sentence) — 30 minutes
- Round 3 (anchor + content) — 60 minutes
- Read the Q&A bench cards once — 15 minutes

**Day 2:**
- Round 3 again — 45 minutes
- Round 4 (timed) twice — 30 minutes + 30 minutes
- Round 5 (stand-and-deliver) once — 20 minutes
- Walk-through of the cheat sheet — 10 minutes

Day-of: one slow read of the cheat sheet in the morning. One in the afternoon. Take the stage.

## The deeper point

A moot is not memorisation. It is **trained recall under pressure.** Lawyers do it for moot court because they know the room will throw the script away — questions interrupt, judges press on points, time gets compressed. You prepare for the conditions, not the conditions you wish for.

This talk has been pressure-tested through three external reviews (two Grok, two ChatGPT). The wording is now harder to defeat without being any smaller. Your job at the lectern is not to deliver the perfect version of the talk that exists on disk. Your job is to deliver the talk *as it survives contact with the room.*

The anchor phrases hold the line when contact gets noisy. Trust them.
